A decentralized ecosystem needs a way to make decisions. If one company decides
the rules, the ecosystem is not really open. This page explains how Orivon is
governed: the layers of decision making, the open standards process, and the
role of the Orivon Foundation.

## The governance problem

Every ecosystem makes decisions: what a standard should say, how a naming
dispute is settled, how scoring rules evolve. The question is who decides. The
worst outcome would be to replace one platform's rule with another platform's
rule. Governance exists to make the rules belong to the community.

## Layers of governance

Governance in the ecosystem works at three levels, each with a clear scope:

<CardGrid columns={3}>
  <Card title="Technical standards">
    How objects, identities, modules and the runtime behave. Decided through
    an open process where anyone can propose and comment.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Community decisions">
    Priorities, disputes and the direction of the ecosystem. Decided by
    participants through structured, transparent processes.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The Foundation">
    Stewardship: funding, coordination and the legal entity that holds shared
    assets. Accountable to the community.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## The open standards process

Standards are the source of truth, so their process must be the most
transparent part of the system. The process follows a clear path:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Propose">
    Anyone can propose a change or a new standard. Proposals are public and
    include the problem and the design.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Discuss">
    The community comments, challenges and improves the proposal. No single
    company fast tracks its own ideas.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Ratify">
    A proposal is accepted when it meets the ecosystem's criteria: technical
    soundness, backward compatibility and community support.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Adopt">
    Implementations adopt the standard, and the ecosystem tests it in the real
    world.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Because standards are what make everything interoperable, this process is
deliberately slower than shipping a feature. Slowness here is a feature: it is
what prevents one participant from changing the rules for everyone.

## Disputes and content decisions

Decentralized systems still need answers to hard questions: who owns a disputed
name? What happens to clearly illegal content? The ecosystem separates the
infrastructure from the rules:

- **The infrastructure stays neutral.** The naming system and storage do not
  decide what is allowed.
- **Communities set rules.** Namespaces and communities set their own policies
  within the standards.
- **Disputes have a path.** Governance provides a transparent dispute process
  with published decisions and appeals.

<Callout type="info" title="Did you know?">
  The separation of infrastructure and rules is what keeps the ecosystem
  replaceable. Because content is stored on neutral infrastructure, communities
  can leave one namespace and start another without losing the web itself.
</Callout>

## The Orivon Foundation

The Orivon Foundation is the legal and organizational steward of the ecosystem.
Its roles are deliberately limited so it serves the community rather than
commanding it:

<CardGrid columns={2}>
  <Card title="Hold shared assets">
    The Foundation holds assets the ecosystem needs to survive, such as
    canonical registries and funding.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Coordinate work">
    It organizes contributions, grants and research across independent teams.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Protect the mission">
    It defends the neutrality and openness of the ecosystem in legal and
    policy settings.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Stay accountable">
    Its budget and decisions are public, and its leadership is accountable to
    the community.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

The Foundation's plans, including the community DAO and reward structures, are
documented as planned work under [Future Products](/docs/ecosystem/future-products)
and the upcoming Foundation section.

## How you participate

Governance is not a spectator sport. Anyone can participate:

<CardGrid columns={2}>
  <Card title="Propose and comment">
    The standards process is open to every participant.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Run a namespace">
    Communities can operate their own namespaces with their own rules.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Contribute work">
    Build modules, infrastructure or research that the ecosystem needs.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Vote in the community">
    Decisions about priorities and direction go through structured community
    processes.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>
