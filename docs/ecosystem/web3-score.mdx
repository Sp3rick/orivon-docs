The Web3 Score is a trust signal for the web. It is a transparent, verifiable
score that tells users how trustworthy, decentralized and well behaved a
website, application or module is. The score is shown natively in the browser,
next to the connection information. This page explains what the score measures,
how it is computed, and how to read it critically.

## The problem the score solves

On the classic web, trust is invisible. Users judge a site by its design, its
domain, or the padlock icon, which only says the connection is encrypted, not
that the site is honest. Scams, fake stores and phishing pages all have valid
certificates and polished design.

The ecosystem needs users to make better decisions without doing security
research on every click. The Web3 Score is an attempt to make trust visible and
auditable.

## What the score measures

The score is not a single opinion. It combines dimensions that together describe
how a site behaves:

<CardGrid columns={2}>
  <Card title="Decentralization">
    Does the site depend on a single server, or does it run on decentralized
    infrastructure?
  </Card>
  <Card title="Ownership transparency">
    Is there a verifiable identity behind the site, and how long has it existed?
  </Card>
  <Card title="Safety behavior">
    Does the site avoid obvious abuse: malware, excessive permissions, deceptive
    patterns?
  </Card>
  <Card title="Trackers and data">
    How much does the site try to track or extract from visitors?
  </Card>
  <Card title="Reputation signals">
    What do independent curators and validators say about the site?
  </Card>
  <Card title="Consistency">
    Does the site behave the same way over time, or does it change behavior
    after being trusted?
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## How the score is computed

The score is assembled from verifiable inputs, not a private algorithm. The key
idea is **transparency about inputs, not one secret number**:

1. **Signals are collected.** Validators and curators publish their assessments
   as signed, addressable records, the same way content is published.
2. **Inputs are public.** Anyone can inspect the evidence behind a score.
3. **The combination is deterministic.** Given the same inputs, the same score
   is computed, so no one can secretly change results.
4. **The user can drill in.** Clicking a score shows its dimensions and the
   evidence for each.

## How users see it

In the browser, the score appears in the address bar area, alongside the usual
connection details. A click opens a breakdown showing each dimension and the
evidence behind it. The score is always visible, never hidden behind a paywall
or a warning dialog.

<Callout type="warning" title="Watch out">
  The score is a signal, not a guarantee. A high score means the public evidence
  is good, not that a site can never be abused. A low score is a strong reason
  to be careful, but legitimate new sites may score low until they build
  evidence.
</Callout>

## What the score is not

It helps to be explicit about the score's limits:

<CardGrid columns={2}>
  <Card title="Not a rating of quality">
    A well scored site can still have a product you dislike. The score measures
    trust behavior, not taste.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Not a guarantee">
    No reputation system can predict the future. It reflects current evidence.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Not anonymous power">
    Because inputs are public and the combination is deterministic, no single
    party controls the score.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Not static">
    Scores update as evidence changes. A site can repair its behavior and
    rebuild its score.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## The score and governance

Scoring rules must evolve as attackers adapt. The standards behind the score
are maintained through the same open
[Governance](/docs/ecosystem/governance) process as everything else, so the
community, not a company, decides how trust is measured.
