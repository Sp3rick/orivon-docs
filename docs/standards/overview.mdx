Orivon is a protocol, a browser and a growing ecosystem. Protocols need
specifications: written, versioned, reviewable documents that define how things
work so that anyone can implement, audit and extend them. This section is the
home of those documents, the Orivon Specifications (OSP) series.

The goal is to feel like a mature engineering ecosystem: Ethereum has EIPs,
Bitcoin has BIPs, the web has W3C and WHATWG documents, and the internet has
IETF RFCs. Orivon has OSPs. This page explains what that means and how to find
your way around.

## What standards are

A standard is a written contract between implementers. It says what a component
MUST do, what it SHOULD do, and what it MAY do, in terms precise enough that two
independent implementations agree without talking to each other.

Standards are not documentation of one implementation. They are the common
ground that many implementations build on. The distinction is central to this
section, and [Why Standards Matter](/docs/standards/why-standards-matter)
explains it in depth.

## Why Orivon uses open specifications

Orivon commits to open specifications for practical, not philosophical,
reasons:

<CheckList
  items={[
    'Interoperability: any compliant implementation can talk to any other.',
    'Auditability: security and privacy claims can be reviewed by anyone.',
    'Third party development: teams can build modules, tools and services without private access.',
    'Stability: a published version is a promise that implementers can rely on.',
    'Community collaboration: decisions are made in the open, not in one company.',
  ]}
/>

The [specification registry](/docs/standards/specification-registry) is the
single place to look up what is defined today, and the
[lifecycle](/docs/standards/specification-lifecycle) describes how a document
moves from idea to accepted standard.

## How standards evolve

Standards change slowly and deliberately. Every document in this section moves
through the same lifecycle:

  <Flow column>
    <DiagramNode accent>Idea</DiagramNode>
    <FlowArrowDown />
    <DiagramNode>Draft</DiagramNode>
    <FlowArrowDown />
    <DiagramNode>Discussion</DiagramNode>
    <FlowArrowDown />
    <DiagramNode>Review</DiagramNode>
    <FlowArrowDown />
    <DiagramNode>Accepted</DiagramNode>
    <FlowArrowDown />
    <DiagramNode>Implemented</DiagramNode>
    <FlowArrowDown />
    <DiagramNode dim>Deprecated</DiagramNode>
    <FlowArrowDown />
    <DiagramNode dim>Archived</DiagramNode>
  </Flow>
</Diagram>

A specification only becomes **Accepted** after review, and only becomes
authoritative when the reference implementation ships it. Nothing in this
section is treated as finished before that. The
[Specification Lifecycle](/docs/standards/specification-lifecycle) page covers
every stage in detail.

## How contributors participate

Anyone can contribute, and the process is documented end to end:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Propose">
    Open a proposal describing the idea, its goals and its non-goals.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Draft">
    Convert the proposal into an OSP document using the
    [template](/docs/standards/specification-template).
  </Step>
  <Step title="Review">
    Submit for public review. The draft is revised through discussion.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Accept">
    Editors merge the accepted version into the
    [registry](/docs/standards/specification-registry).
  </Step>
</Steps>

The [Writing Specifications](/docs/standards/writing-specifications) page
explains how to write the document itself, including the RFC terminology that
every OSP uses.

## Current specification status

The standards program is young, and that is reflected in the status of every
document:

<CardGrid columns={3}>
  <Card title="15 in draft" to="/docs/standards/draft-specifications">
    All fifteen OSP-001 to OSP-015 documents are public drafts.
  </Card>
  <Card title="0 accepted" to="/docs/standards/accepted-specifications">
    Acceptance requires review and a reference implementation. None have shipped yet.
  </Card>
  <Card title="0 deprecated" to="/docs/standards/deprecated-specifications">
    The deprecation process is defined and documented, ready for future use.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

Every draft is clearly labeled **Draft** on its page. Nothing is presented as
finished that is not. The [Compliance](/docs/standards/compliance) and
[Conformance Testing](/docs/standards/conformance-testing) pages define what
"done" will mean.

<Callout type="warning" title="Status clarity">
  An OSP in Draft is not a guarantee. Drafts describe intent and direction; only
  an Accepted specification is a promise. Read the status before relying on any
  document in this section.
</Callout>
