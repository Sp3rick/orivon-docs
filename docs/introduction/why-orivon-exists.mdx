## The internet drifted from its promise

The web was designed as an open, decentralized information system. Today, most of what
people experience online is controlled by a handful of platforms that decide what you see,
what you can do, and what happens to your data. The gap between the web's original promise
and its current reality is the reason Orivon exists.

## The problems with today's internet

### 1. Centralization

Almost every major service is a server farm owned by a corporation. This concentrates
power, and with it: control over access, incentives to monetize attention, and single
points of failure for outages, censorship and hacks alike.

### 2. Privacy erosion

Free services are often paid for with data. Tracking, profiling and ad-driven business
models treat the user as the product. The browser, your front door to the internet, is
itself a surveillance vector by default.

### 3. Fragmentation

Your identity is a hundred logins. Your data lives in a hundred silos. Your digital money
is managed by a hundred apps. Every new service asks you to start over. The result is a
web of disconnected, incompatible systems.

### 4. Installing many applications

Modern software has become a pile of separate applications: a wallet here, a messenger
there, a storage client somewhere else. Each needs installation, updates, permissions and
trust. Users juggle dozens of apps for what should be a single, coherent experience.

<Callout type="warning" title="The cost is real">
  These aren't abstract concerns. They are the everyday experience of billions of people:
  data breaches, identity theft, rent-seeking fees, walled gardens and software that works
  against its user.
</Callout>

## Why browsers need to evolve

The browser is the most important software on most devices. It is how people read, shop,
work, bank and socialize. Yet its role has stayed largely unchanged for two decades: a
viewer for documents and pages.

As software moves online, the browser becomes more than a viewer. It becomes the
**operating environment** for the next generation of applications. That shift demands new
capabilities:

- a **runtime** that runs applications, not just pages,
- **native identity and payment** support, instead of hacks,
- **sandboxing and security** that users can actually understand,
- and **standards** that keep the platform open and neutral.

## Why Web3 deserves native browser support

Web3 (decentralized networks for money, identity, data and applications) offers a
genuine alternative to the centralized status quo. But today it is held back by
usability:

- Users install extensions with unclear security boundaries.
- Each chain and each dApp reinvents onboarding.
- Private keys are dangerously close to phishing and malware.
- Decentralized names and storage are invisible to the browser.

The web platform **already solved this class of problem once**: it standardized URLs, TLS,
cookies and JavaScript so that any site works in any browser. Web3 needs the same
treatment: made native, specified openly, and safe by default.

That is the gap Orivon exists to close.

## A browser for the next generation of the web

| Problem today | Orivon's response |
| --- | --- |
| Centralized control | Open standards, modular ecosystem, community governance |
| Eroded privacy | Privacy-preserving defaults, user-owned data |
| Fragmentation | One coherent platform for identity, value and apps |
| App overload | Applications as websites, with no installs and no silos |
| Web3 as an afterthought | Web3 as a first-class, native browser capability |
